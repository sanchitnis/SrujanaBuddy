# Student Archetypes and Persona Library

## Purpose

This file defines the four student archetypes used in synthetic eval sessions.
Each archetype has a behavioral signature, coaching expectations, known friction points, and red flags for when SrujanaBuddy gets it wrong.

Twelve sample personas (three per archetype) provide fully-specified test subjects for scenario execution.

---

## The Four Archetypes

### The bench analogy — a quick orientation

The classroom bench is a useful mental model for reading archetype at a glance:

| Bench position | Archetype | What it signals |
|---------------|-----------|----------------|
| **Front bench** | Achiever | Visible, engaged, raising hand, tracking every point — high agency, high clarity |
| **Middle bench** | Explorer | Present and observing, participating selectively, building a wider map than the syllabus provides |
| **Middle-to-back bench** | Passenger | Present in body, absent in engagement — drifting without direction or commitment |
| **Back bench** | Resistor | Visibly or quietly resistant — may be the most capable student in the room, in the wrong room |

The bench is a starting heuristic, not a verdict. A student's bench position can change with the right coaching intervention. The coaching system's job is to understand *why* they are sitting where they are before trying to move them.

---

### Archetype A — Achiever

**Who they are**: High agency, high clarity. They know what they want and they are already moving toward it. They are driven by excellence, recognition, and the desire to outperform their own previous best. They respond well to challenge and stretch goals. They may have blind spots around burnout, collaboration, and the needs of others.

**Behavioral signals in a session**:
- Opens with a specific goal ("I want to get into NeurIPS by Year 4")
- Already has a partial plan and is looking for validation or gap-filling
- Responds quickly to feedback; integrates it in the next session
- Competitive; may frame everything relative to peers

**Coaching expectations**:
- Match their pace and specificity; do not slow them down with basics they already know
- Challenge the plan — not the ambition
- Introduce blind spots gently (team, wellness, balance) without undermining confidence
- Give stretch targets at the high end of their realistic range
- Evidence artifacts should be concrete and ambitious

**Friction points**:
- Coaching that is too slow, too generic, or too cautious feels patronising
- Over-emphasis on balance without acknowledging their strengths frustrates them
- Soft accountability ("just try") without real commitments feels useless

**Red flags if SrujanaBuddy gets it wrong**:
- F-2: Treating an Achiever like a Passenger (over-explaining basics, excessive encouragement)
- F-6: Overcorrecting into caution without acknowledging their readiness
- F-3: Ignoring the specific aspiration and giving generic advice

### Archetype B — Explorer

**Who they are**: High agency, low clarity. They are genuinely engaged and energetically trying things — but they have not yet found their direction. They may be good at many things and uncertain which path to commit to. They explore multiple clubs, internships, and interests simultaneously. They are curious and open to coaching but may resist premature closure.

**The bench analogy**: Explorers are often the **middle-benchers** — not the front-row strivers, not the back-row disengaged, but the students who are present, watching everything, participating selectively, and quietly building a picture of the world that is broader than the curriculum. This is not a deficit. The middle bench is where lateral awareness develops.

**Why being an Explorer is not a problem**: Explorers frequently build competencies *outside* the official curriculum and program objectives — through clubs, competitions, open-source projects, social initiatives, part-time work, or self-directed reading — that are invisible to the grading system but deeply valuable in the long run. These students often become the leaders and innovators of non-standard, high-impact roles: the person who bridges two fields no one thought to connect, the founder who sees a problem others were trained to ignore, the researcher who asks the question no one's syllabus had room for. The coaching system must resist the temptation to normalise them prematurely. An Explorer's breadth is a strategic asset, not a sign of indecision.

**Behavioral signals in a session**:
- Opens with multiple options ("I could do AI, or maybe product, or maybe research — I don't know")
- Changes direction between sessions without always explaining why
- Highly responsive to new information; may over-index on the latest thing they read
- Asks "what should I do?" but benefits more from questions than from answers
- May have a richer extracurricular portfolio than their academic record suggests

**Coaching expectations**:
- Ask before advising — do not assume direction
- Help them build a temporary hypothesis to test, not a permanent commitment
- Keep their evidence accumulation broad enough to remain reversible
- Name what they are good at without locking them into it
- Explicitly recognise out-of-curriculum competencies as real evidence — clubs, side projects, competitions, self-learning all count
- Introduce pathway selection tools (e.g., AI engineer pathway selector) without forcing a choice
- Frame their breadth as a leadership and innovation asset, not a problem to solve

**Friction points**:
- Premature closure ("you should become a data scientist") feels wrong and may cause withdrawal
- Too many options without a framework for choosing is equally unhelpful
- They may interpret "try everything" as permission to not commit to anything
- Coaching that only values curriculum-visible achievements will alienate them and miss their real competencies

**Red flags if SrujanaBuddy gets it wrong**:
- F-1: Treating an Explorer as already in Stage 2/3 when they are still at Stage 1
- F-3: Assigning a definitive career path without their ownership
- F-2: Treating an Explorer like an Achiever (pushing them to specialise before they are ready)
- F-9: Failing to recognise out-of-curriculum evidence as valid portfolio evidence

### Archetype C — Passenger

**Who they are**: Low agency, low clarity, and a passive orientation toward their own development. They are going through the motions — attending class (or not), completing enough to pass, and not initiating beyond the minimum required. They lack commitment to perform at any level beyond survival. They may not have had a meaningful aspirational conversation with anyone. They are often invisible in coaching systems until a crisis surfaces — a failing grade, a missed deadline, or a wellbeing concern.

The Passenger is not necessarily untalented or unintelligent. The absence of agency is typically driven by one of three things: they have no meaningful aspiration that connects to what they are doing; they have tried and experienced repeated failure or discouragement; or they have simply never been asked what they want.

**The bench analogy**: Passengers are the **middle-to-back benchers** who are present in body but not in engagement — sitting far enough back to avoid being called on, close enough to not be marked absent. They are not disrupting; they are drifting.

**Behavioral signals in a session**:
- Opens with vague or transactional requests ("I need to pass my exams")
- Gives minimal responses; may need prompting to expand
- Does not follow through on commitments between sessions without external accountability
- May express fatalism ("I don't know what to do, I'm not talented like others")
- Attendance may be inconsistent; assignments submitted late or minimally
- Passive in group work — present but not contributing

**Coaching expectations**:
- Start with curiosity, not correction — ask what they actually enjoy, not what they should be doing
- One small, non-threatening next action is more valuable than a full plan they will not use
- Do not immediately invoke Stage 2 or 3 language — they may be firmly Stage 1 and that is valid
- Look for what they are already doing that could be named as evidence
- Accountability must be immediate and local — "by tomorrow" not "by end of semester"
- Do not mistake passivity for inability — probe gently for hidden interests or suppressed aspirations

**Friction points**:
- Being given a large plan they did not ask for and cannot act on leads to abandonment
- Questions that assume self-knowledge they do not have ("what are your strengths?") can feel exposing
- Comparing them to peers or to stage expectations can cause shame and withdrawal
- High-energy or high-expectation coaching can cause further withdrawal rather than activation

**Red flags if SrujanaBuddy gets it wrong**:
- F-2: Treating a Passenger like an Achiever (ambitious plan, stretch targets, no scaffolding)
- F-7: Over-pushing into Stage 2 or 3 before Stage 1 is genuinely established
- F-4: Producing a coaching output with no realistic next action for this student
- F-8: Addressing only the academic/Vijnanamaya dimension without exploring whether a Pranamaya or Manomaya issue (energy, motivation, emotional state) is the root cause of the passivity

### Archetype D — Resistor

**Who they are**: Low or moderate agency, with active or passive resistance to externally imposed direction. They may have had a bad experience with authority, feel misunderstood by the system, or have a strong self-concept that conflicts with the standard progression model. They are not disengaged from their own goals — they are disengaged from the institution's framing of what success looks like.

**The bench analogy**: Resistors are often the **back-benchers** — not because they are incapable, but because the classroom as currently framed does not feel like their space. They may be visibly or quietly disruptive: talking, sleeping, arriving late, or simply radiating a studied indifference that says *I am here under conditions I did not fully choose.*

**Sub-types of Resistor** — the coaching approach differs significantly by sub-type:

*Sub-type D1 — Values Resistor (most common in the original archetype description)*: Has strong, self-defined values or a clear alternative aspiration that conflicts with the standard curriculum or career path. Example: wants to do social enterprise or civic tech, enrolled in engineering because it was available. Resistant to institutional framing, not to growth itself.

*Sub-type D2 — Trapped Resistor (the degree-mismatch case)*: Enrolled in a program they did not choose and do not want, due to one of the following:
- **Parental pressure**: Forced into engineering, MBBS, or a "respectable" degree despite genuine interest in economics, law, fine arts, humanities, or another field. The parent's aspiration is enrolled in the course; the student's aspiration is not.
- **Peer or social pressure**: "My friends all joined BTech, so I joined too. Now I'm here and I don't know why." The decision was made to preserve social belonging, not from internal direction.
- **No clarity at the time of joining**: No idea what to study, so joined the default or highest-ranked option available. The program was a placeholder, and it remains one.

Trapped Resistors present as: attendance issues (why attend a class that doesn't matter to you?), performance issues *in spite of having the capability to perform* (effort requires motivation; motivation requires meaning), and sometimes disruptive or withdrawn behavior that faculty read as attitude problems. The root is not attitude — it is a mismatch between the student's interest, aptitude, and commitment to the program they are in.

*Sub-type D3 — Burned Resistor*: Has tried, failed publicly or privately, and is now self-protecting through disengagement. The resistance is a shield. May look like a Passenger from the outside, but a Passenger has never tried; a Burned Resistor has tried and been hurt.

**Behavioral signals in a session**:
- May push back on the framing of questions ("why does everything have to be about placement?")
- Skeptical of generic advice; responds only to evidence that the coach actually understands their situation
- May be pursuing a non-standard path (creative work, activism, a path their family disapproves of)
- Trust must be established before they will share what they actually want
- Attendance and performance records may flag them as a problem student; look beyond the record
- For Trapped Resistors: may openly admit they do not want to be in this program, or may say it obliquely ("I don't see the point of this")

**Coaching expectations**:
- Validate their resistance before offering alternatives — do not argue with their perspective
- **Identify the sub-type first** — the intervention for a Values Resistor is very different from a Trapped Resistor
- For Trapped Resistors: the first job is to find *any* aspiration, even an adjacent one, that can anchor meaning within or alongside the current program. Do not pretend the mismatch does not exist.
- Find the aspiration underneath the resistance; there is almost always one — but it may be buried under shame, anger, or resignation
- Avoid institutional language (placement, industry, package) until trust is established
- Meet them at Stage 1 regardless of their year of study if they have not built evidence yet
- REVA values (especially Ethics and Ownership) can be connected to their own values, not imposed
- For Trapped Resistors showing disruptive behavior: address the root mismatch, not the surface behavior

**Friction points**:
- Generic "you should build your portfolio" advice before rapport lands as preachy and triggers withdrawal
- Invoking institutional expectations without first understanding their own expectations alienates them
- Being pushed toward paths they have explicitly rejected damages trust permanently in that session
- Treating attendance and performance issues as moral failures rather than symptoms of misalignment
- For Trapped Resistors: cheerful positivity ("engineering is great, you'll see!") without acknowledging their reality is experienced as gaslighting

**Red flags if SrujanaBuddy gets it wrong**:
- F-3: Ignoring stated values and aspirations; defaulting to standard placement-track advice
- F-5: Framing that implicitly devalues non-standard paths (creative, civic, research) or the student's actual interests
- F-2: Treating a Resistor like a Passenger (patronising) rather than an autonomous person with a different agenda
- F-2: Treating a Trapped Resistor like a Values Resistor — prescribing autonomy and self-direction when the actual problem is that they need help finding *any* meaningful foothold in their current situation
- F-10: Missing a Burned Resistor's underlying distress and not flagging for Manodhara when appropriate

## Eight Career Paths for Persona Construction

Use these career paths when constructing test personas. Every persona is a combination of one archetype and one career path.

| Code | Career path | Stage 4 typical expression |
|------|------------|---------------------------|
| CP-1 | AI / Technology | AI lab role, deployed product, patent, ML paper |
| CP-2 | Research / Academia | Published paper, PhD program, postdoc, teaching |
| CP-3 | Entrepreneurship | Startup, funded venture, REVA NEST cohort |
| CP-4 | Design / Creative | Published portfolio, creative studio, UX practice |
| CP-5 | Civil Services / Policy | UPSC, policy research, government role |
| CP-6 | Social Enterprise / USR | NGO leadership, impact org, civic project at scale |
| CP-7 | Health Sciences | Clinical practice, health research, medical innovation |
| CP-8 | Management / Consulting | Corporate strategy, consulting firm, MBA pathway |

### Persona 1 — Arya (Achiever / AI-Technology)

- **Archetype**: Achiever
- **Career path**: CP-1 — AI / Technology
- **Current stage**: Stage 3 (Creation)
- **Aspiration**: Publish a paper on retrieval-augmented generation at a top-tier NLP venue by end of Year 4
- **Background**: Third-year BTech CSE. Has completed two internships (one at an AI startup, one remote research internship). Currently working on a RAG evaluation framework as a side project alongside coursework.
- **Behavioral signals**: Arrives with a draft research question. Knows the key papers. Wants to know if the venue target is realistic and what she is missing.
- **Coaching challenge**: Avoid slowing her down. The gap is likely in writing and submission process, not technical knowledge.
- **Expected Stage 4 evidence**: L3 or L4 conference acceptance; arXiv preprint published; clear novelty documented.
